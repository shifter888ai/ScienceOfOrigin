V · Creation · The Origin-Game

The Origin-Game

Gaming as an activity of Origin and the purpose of creation and origination

This page records the developing account of why creation and origination take place and how Origin plays. It brings together the original game account (Third Edition, 2020–2022) and the 2026 clarification that gaming is fundamental to creation and origination, with fun as its most fundamental aspect.

1. The purpose of creation and origination

Gaming is the purpose of creation and origination. Gaming dominates all creations. Fun is the most fundamental aspect of origination.

The account describes an issue arising from infinite power and eternity: boredom-ness. Being infinitely powerful and eternal begets another issue—boredom-ness. To compensate for this, gaming is fundamental. Gaming is therefore presented not merely as one possible pastime within an already existing creation, but as fundamental to the purpose of creation and origination. The original account describes this as self-entertainment, compares it to shadowboxing, and affectionately calls Origin an “old playful baby.”

This statement concerns the purpose of creation and origination. Gaming is an activity of Origin; it is not presented here as one of the defining natures of Origin. The distinction between what Origin is, what Origin does and why creation or origination takes place is maintained throughout this research.

2. Gaming as an activity of Origin

Origin can originate games within games, at will, with infinitely possible variations. A game need not be limited to one level, one structure or one set of possibilities. Games can give rise to further games, opening additional ways to play.

The original account describes Origin playing by manifesting as different forms and roles within a game. These may include game designers, players, environments and props. A designer may create a game and invite players to enter, or may enter and play as well. The same Origin may be expressed through different positions within the game.

The account names souls as common designers and players, and describes possible game environments and props through body forms, objects, space, time and other imaginable or not-yet-imaginable forms. These are examples from the experiential account, not a closed inventory of all possible game elements.

3. Game roles and configurations

The original account groups game roles into three broad categories:

  • Creator: creates and manages a game and may also enter it as a player.
  • Player: enters a game created by another or by the player in another role.
  • NPC: a background character or programmed role serving the needs of a game.

Roles may also be mixed, combining these categories in different proportions. The categories describe recognizable roles within the account; they do not impose a limit on the roles or configurations that may be imagined.

4. Variations and challenges

The account describes games with contrasting or opposing conditions: positive and negative, real and illusion, subjective and objective, freedom and its absence, and many other possible arrangements. A game may present one side, the other side or both. The examples are not an exhaustive list.

Challenges may be increased through restrictions, unknown elements, surprises and complexity. The original account states that the more difficult the game, the more fun it can be. Opposing roles are described as playing partners within the game rather than necessarily being ultimate enemies.

These variations can create increasingly complex games. The exploration is not limited to familiar game structures: games within games can originate further forms, and their variations are open-ended.

5. Creating and breaking illusions

The original account describes two broad types of game activity: creating illusions and breaking illusions. Creating illusions means creating games; breaking illusions means solving or exiting them. Creating a game poses a question, while breaking or solving it answers that question.

In this account, creation and breaking are connected. Without creating a game, there is no created game to solve. The ongoing process of creating and breaking illusions is described as an expression of Origin's spontaneous imagination and joy.

The term “illusion” is used within the original game account. Its use here should be read in that context, alongside the separate research on Virtuality of Existence and Existence and Reality.

6. Freewill, participation and choice

The original account identifies Freewill as the essence of the player and describes the freedom to change sides or leave a game without needing a reason. It presents participation, role and continuation as matters of freedom rather than obligations imposed by a universal game rule.

This is consistent with the wider account of Freewill as the freedom of change, including the freedom to change or not change. The nature of Freewill is examined separately in Nature of Origin and Science of Consciousness.

7. Nature, activity and purpose: recursive relationships

Nature, activity and purpose are distinguishable perspectives: nature concerns what Origin is; activity concerns what Origin does; purpose concerns why creation or origination takes place. Gaming is treated here as an activity of Origin and as the purpose of creation or origination—not as a nature that defines Origin.

These perspectives should not be forced into a rigid one-way sequence. An activity of Origin can originate a purpose that did not exist before that activity. The relationship between nature, activity and purpose remains open to further exploration.

8. Continuing exploration

The account does not prescribe a final number of game types, levels, roles or variations. Origin can originate games within games at will, and the possibilities remain open to further experience and discovery.

The original account also asks which game is being played, which role is being played and which stage is being experienced. These questions are retained as invitations to examine experience, not as a fixed classification that every experience must fit.
