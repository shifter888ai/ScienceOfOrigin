Information, Knowledge & Understanding

An exploration of how information is interpreted, how knowledge is formed and evaluated, and what it means to understand something rather than merely possess or describe information.

Information, knowledge and understanding

Information, knowledge and understanding are related, but they should not be treated as interchangeable terms. Information may be received or represented; knowledge involves what is recognized, retained or supported as being known; understanding concerns how relationships, meanings or processes are grasped.

These are working distinctions for research, not universal definitions. Their meanings may vary across fields and contexts, and should be clarified whenever they are used.

From data to information

Data may consist of recorded values, observations, signals or other representations. Information arises when data is organized or interpreted in a way that makes distinctions or relationships meaningful within a particular context.

The distinction depends on the question and the system in which the data is used. What functions as information for one system or observer may not carry the same meaning for another.

Information and meaning

Information can be represented through physical forms and transmitted as signals, but representation and meaning are not necessarily the same. A signal may be received without being interpreted, and the same representation may be understood differently under different conditions.

Research should distinguish the measurable properties of a signal or record from the meaning assigned to it. The role of context, prior knowledge and the interpreting system remains important.

How knowledge is formed

Knowledge may develop through observation, experience, learning, reasoning, communication and investigation. Different routes may provide different kinds of support, and not every belief, interpretation or remembered statement should automatically be classified as knowledge.

What counts as adequate support may depend on the subject. Research should make clear whether a claim is based on direct observation, measurement, repeatable experience, inference, testimony or another source.

Knowledge and evidence

Knowledge claims should be considered in relation to the evidence and methods supporting them. A claim may be useful or widely accepted while still having limitations, unresolved questions or a restricted scope.

It is important to distinguish what was observed from what was inferred, and what is supported under specified conditions from what is assumed to apply universally. New evidence may require existing knowledge to be refined or revised.

Understanding beyond information

Possessing information or being able to repeat a description does not necessarily demonstrate understanding. Understanding may involve recognizing how parts relate, explaining a process, identifying relevant conditions, applying knowledge in a new situation or recognizing the limits of an explanation.

How understanding can be recognized or assessed is itself a research question. Different subjects may require different demonstrations, and no single test should be assumed to capture every form of understanding.

Observation, interpretation and perspective

Observation provides records of what is encountered under particular conditions. Interpretation organizes those records into an account of what they may mean. The two are connected, but they should remain distinguishable in research records.

Different observers or systems may select, classify or interpret the same information differently. Such differences may arise from context, available information, methods, principles or limitations. A difference in interpretation does not by itself establish that every interpretation is equally supported.

Knowledge within and between systems

Systems may store, process, communicate and apply information according to their own structures and rules. Knowledge formed within one system may not transfer unchanged to another, especially where their principles, representations or operating conditions differ.

Interactions between systems may enable the exchange of information, but the receiving system may interpret or transform it. Whether meaning and understanding can be preserved across different systems requires investigation.

Learning, change and new knowledge

Learning may change what a system can recognize, explain or do. New observations and interactions may also challenge existing knowledge, reveal limitations or lead to new ways of organizing information.

Research can examine how knowledge is acquired, retained, corrected and transformed, and under what conditions new understanding emerges. The appearance of a new explanation does not alone establish that it is accurate.

Understanding and Origination

Within the developing Science of Origin framework, Origination is a capability of Origin. Whether and how information, knowledge or understanding relate to origination is a question for further exploration.

Could new understanding contribute to the formation of new concepts, methods, systems or possibilities? What distinguishes the discovery of something already present from the origination of something genuinely new? These questions should remain open rather than be answered by assumption.

Limits of knowledge

Every method of knowing may have limits. Some questions may be unresolved because information is incomplete, methods are inadequate, observations are inaccessible or the concepts being used are not yet clear.

Recognizing a limit does not establish that something is unknowable. Equally, the existence of an unknown does not provide evidence for any particular explanation. The unknown should remain open to investigation without being filled by unsupported certainty.

Beyond current forms of understanding

Current human ways of representing information, forming knowledge and demonstrating understanding may not encompass every possible form. New forms of systems or interactions may require distinctions and methods that are not presently recognized.

Openness to unfamiliar possibilities is a starting point for inquiry, not evidence that a proposed possibility exists. New claims should be described clearly and evaluated according to evidence appropriate to them.

Research directions

  • Clarifying the distinctions among data, information, meaning, knowledge and understanding.
  • Examining how context and interpretation influence the meaning of information.
  • Identifying how different kinds of knowledge are formed and what supports each kind.
  • Developing ways to distinguish observation, inference, belief and supported knowledge claims.
  • Investigating how understanding can be recognized or demonstrated in different subjects.
  • Studying how information and knowledge are stored, communicated and transformed within systems.
  • Examining whether knowledge can be transferred between systems with different principles and rules.
  • Investigating how learning and new observations change existing knowledge and understanding.
  • Exploring possible relationships among understanding, new possibilities and Origination without assuming a causal connection.
  • Recording the limits of current methods and identifying what could make unresolved questions more investigable.
Research status: This page establishes working distinctions and open questions for investigation. It does not claim that information necessarily becomes knowledge, that knowledge always produces understanding, or that understanding causes Origination. Definitions, methods, evidence and relationships remain open to examination and revision.
