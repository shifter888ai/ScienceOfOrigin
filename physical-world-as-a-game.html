IV · Origin Models

The Physical World as a Game

Exploring the physical world as a bounded game within the proposed Fake Origin model, and examining how its apparent independence and completeness may be experienced from within.

Scope

This subject examines a specific proposition within the developing Science of Origin framework: that the physical world may be understood as a constructed game — a manifestation with its own boundaries, conditions, rules and possibilities — rather than assumed to be the totality of existence.

Within the proposed Fake Origin model, the physical world is considered a relatively recent and comparatively small game: a fresh, bounded domain within a wider context of existence. Its apparent physicality, separation and self-containment are central features to investigate. These are framework propositions, not independently verified findings.

The physical world as a game

Here, game is used in the broad sense developed in Constructed Realities and Games. It does not mean only entertainment, a digital simulation or a computer-generated environment. It refers to an organized domain in which boundaries, rules, conditions and possibilities shape what can occur and how its participants experience it.

In this proposed model, the physical world is one such domain. Its physical structures and processes are treated as features of the game, not as proof that the game is the whole of existence or that nothing exists beyond its boundaries.

A relatively small and recent game

The exploratory model characterizes the physical world as the newest and smallest game within a wider range of possible games or manifestations. The description “fresh baby game” expresses its proposed relative scale, scope and developmental position in that wider context.

These comparisons are not yet defined by a common measure. “Newest” and “smallest” therefore remain qualitative propositions within the model; they should not be mistaken for an established chronology, measured size or demonstrated ranking.

The proposal invites questions about how games or domains may differ in scope, complexity, possibilities, freedom, duration and the capabilities available within them.

Boundaries that make the game convincing

Within the proposed model, the physical world's boundaries are especially clear or strong from the perspective of its participants. These boundaries may shape what can be perceived, accessed, changed or even considered possible from within the domain.

A participant may consequently interpret the limits encountered within the physical world as limits of existence itself. The model proposes that this is a central part of the game's convincing appearance: the boundaries of one domain may be mistaken for the boundaries of everything that exists.

This is distinct from saying that physical boundaries are unreal or ineffective within the physical domain. A boundary may be operationally real and consequential within a game without being an ultimate boundary of existence.

Physicality and apparent independence

The physical world is experienced through stable structures, regularities, constraints and interactions. Within this framework, these characteristics are examined as features of the game that contribute to its appearance as a self-contained physical reality.

The proposed model further explores how a domain may appear independent of any wider source. Its internal consistency and the limits of participants' access may make it difficult to recognize relationships beyond the domain. This connects to Simulated Absolute Independence.

The proposal does not imply that the physical world is unreal in the ordinary sense that it has no effects or consequences. It distinguishes the reality and operation of a domain within its own conditions from the stronger claim that it is the whole or ultimate reality.

Players and perspectives within the game

Participants experience a game from within the conditions available to them. Their observations, knowledge and actions may be shaped by the domain's rules, boundaries, physical capabilities and limited perspectives.

Within the exploratory model, a participant may be told that the physical world is a game and still find its boundaries compelling. Understanding a proposition intellectually does not necessarily remove the practical constraints or the immediate experience of the domain.

The relationship between a participant's perspective and the wider context proposed by the framework is therefore a central research question. The model should not assume that every participant has the same access, understanding or experience.

The game's convincing illusion

The exploratory description of the physical world as a “cheating game” refers to the contrast between the wider relationship proposed by the framework and the appearance presented from within the game. The game is considered convincing because its boundaries and conditions can lead participants to treat the domain as independent and complete.

In this usage, “illusion” concerns the interpretation that the physical domain is the totality of existence or is absolutely independent. It does not deny that physical experiences occur or that actions within the domain have consequences. Whether the proposed wider relationship is correct remains an open question requiring appropriate grounds for examination.

Relationship to True Origin and Fake Origin

Within the proposed model, True Origin is the Oneness Origin. Fake Origin is not a second ultimate Origin; it is a manifestation originated from True Origin that imitates Origin and presents relative independence as absolute independence.

The physical world is considered here as a game within that proposed Fake Origin construct. This is a specific application of the model, not a conclusion that follows merely from the existence of physical laws, boundaries, or a coherent physical universe.

The proposed relationship must remain distinct from what can be directly observed about physical phenomena. The framework's interpretation and the evidence available for evaluating it are separate matters.

Research questions

  • What criteria would distinguish a constructed game from a domain that is not constructed in this sense?
  • What is meant by describing the physical world as a game, beyond metaphor or analogy?
  • In what sense could the physical world be the newest or smallest game, and what measures would make those comparisons meaningful?
  • Which physical, informational, experiential or conceptual boundaries define the domain?
  • How can the limits of a domain be distinguished from limits of existence as a whole?
  • How do physical regularities and constraints contribute to the experience of a self-contained reality?
  • What might count as evidence of relationships between the physical domain and a wider context?
  • How do participants' perspectives and access to information shape their understanding of the domain?
  • How does this proposed application relate to Fake Origin, fabricated division and simulated absolute independence?
  • What observations could challenge the proposal, and how could it be investigated without treating its assumptions as evidence for its conclusions?
