II · Nature of Existence

Existence & Reality

Investigating what it means to exist, what makes a reality, how manifestations are experienced, and whether the reality described within a particular system encompasses everything that exists.

The nature of existence

Existence is a central subject of Science of Origin. What does it mean for something to exist? Is existence one condition shared by everything, or can there be different forms or modes of existence?

The project does not settle these questions by definition alone. The meaning of existence, the criteria by which it may be recognized, and the limits of what can be investigated remain open subjects.

It is useful to distinguish the question of whether something exists from questions about what it is, how it came about, how it behaves, and whether an observer can detect it. These questions may be related, but an answer to one does not automatically answer the others.

Existence, origination and manifestation

Within the developing framework, Origin concerns the fundamental source; origination concerns the capability and process through which manifestations come about; and manifestation identifies what comes about.

Existence concerns the status of being, while manifestation concerns what is manifested. Their relationship requires further clarification. Does everything that exists necessarily manifest in some way? Could there be aspects of existence that are not manifested, or are not recognizable through currently available means?

These distinctions help prevent the source, the process, the result and the status of being from being treated as interchangeable concepts. See Origin, Origination and Manifestations.

What is meant by reality?

The word reality can refer to different things: what exists, what is experienced, what can be observed, what is consistently encountered within a system, or the model used to describe these things. Those meanings should be distinguished when making a claim about reality.

A description or model of reality is not necessarily identical to everything it describes. A map, representation or explanation may be useful and internally consistent while remaining partial. Likewise, the limits of an observer's access do not by themselves establish the limits of existence.

Reality within a system

A system may operate according to particular principles, rules, conditions and boundaries. These shape what can occur within it, how its manifestations relate, and what participants or observers can experience.

What is real within a system should be distinguished from the broader question of whether that system encompasses everything that exists. A system's internally consistent account may describe its own conditions accurately without establishing that its rules are universal or that no wider context exists. This connects with the study of Systems and Boundaries and Domains.

This distinction does not make a system's experiences or consequences unreal. Events and interactions may have real effects within the system even while questions about the system's wider context remain open.

Experienced reality and observed reality

Experience and observation provide access to aspects of reality, but they are not automatically complete accounts of it. What an observer experiences or records may depend on perspective, available information, instruments, interpretation and the conditions of observation. This distinction is developed further in Perception and Observation.

Research should distinguish:

  • Experience: what is directly encountered by a participant or observer.
  • Observation: what is recorded through a stated method or instrument.
  • Interpretation: the meaning assigned to an experience or observation.
  • Model: an organized description intended to explain or predict aspects of what is observed.
  • Existence claim: a claim about what is or is not, which may require evidence beyond the fact that something has or has not been observed.

A change in perception or knowledge should not automatically be treated as a change in the thing being observed. Whether observation also affects the observed system is a separate question requiring an appropriate method.

Relative reality and existence as a whole

A reality may be described relative to a particular system, perspective, set of conditions or level of investigation. Such a description can be meaningful without being a complete account of existence as a whole.

For example, rules that consistently govern events within one domain may define that domain's operational reality. They do not, by themselves, establish that the domain is the only reality or that its boundaries are the ultimate boundaries of existence.

The distinction between a local or system-relative account and a broader account is a research question. It should not be used to assume that every system is artificial, simulated or deceptive. Those are more specific claims that require separate examination.

Appearance, illusion and manifestation

Something may appear different from what it is understood to be, or an observer may mistake a limited representation for a complete account. This raises questions about appearance, interpretation and illusion.

Within the developing Science of Origin framework, manifestations are considered imaginations of Origin. From this perspective, the familiar distinction between what is real and what is imagined may need to be examined at a more foundational level. This is a framework proposition, not an established scientific conclusion.

Calling a manifestation an illusion or an imagination should not be taken to mean that it has no existence, that experiences within it have no consequences, or that every apparent feature is false. The terms require precise meanings, and claims about a particular manifestation should be assessed separately.

Multiple forms or domains of reality

If different manifestations or systems can operate according to different principles and rules, it is worth investigating whether they may involve different forms or conditions of reality. Such domains might be distinct, overlapping, connected or capable of interaction.

Differences between domains do not automatically establish absolute separation. Nor does the possibility of interaction establish that their rules or conditions are identical. The nature of any relationship must be examined in context.

The possibility of multiple realities does not, by itself, establish that any particular model of them is true. Clear definitions, criteria and evidence are needed before drawing conclusions.

Origin and reality

Origin is the fundamental source and the central subject of this project. How Origin relates to existence and to the realities of manifestations remains a foundational question.

Do existence and reality describe aspects of manifestation, consequences of origination, or concepts that apply to Origin in a different way? Are familiar ideas such as space, time, physicality and objecthood appropriate descriptions of Origin? The framework does not assume that human categories define the source.

These questions connect this subject with Zero — Idle State of Origin, which explores the proposed idle state of Origin and Infinity before manifestation.

Beyond known existence

Current human knowledge may not encompass every possible form of existence or reality. New observations, methods or conceptual developments may reveal distinctions that are not presently recognized.

Openness to unfamiliar possibilities does not establish that they exist. Research should distinguish what has been directly observed, what is inferred, what is proposed as a possibility and what remains unknown. Neither familiarity nor unfamiliarity is sufficient on its own to determine whether something is possible.

Research directions

  • Clarifying the meanings of existence, reality, experience, observation, interpretation, model and manifestation.
  • Identifying what evidence is relevant to different kinds of existence claims.
  • Investigating whether existence necessarily involves manifestation.
  • Examining how principles, rules, conditions and boundaries shape the reality described within a system.
  • Distinguishing a system's operational reality from claims about existence as a whole.
  • Separating changes in perception or knowledge from changes in the observed system.
  • Investigating the meanings of appearance, representation and illusion without assuming that an illusion has no effects or existence.
  • Exploring possible relationships among different domains of reality, including interaction and apparent separation.
  • Investigating the relationship between Origin, existence, origination and manifested realities.
  • Identifying which questions can be approached through observation, measurement, comparison or experiment, and recording the limits of each method.
