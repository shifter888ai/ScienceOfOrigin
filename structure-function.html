III · Structure

Structure & Function

An exploration of how manifestations and systems are organized, how their components and relationships contribute to what they do, and how structure and function may shape one another.

Structure and function

Structure concerns the arrangement and organization of components, properties and relationships within a manifestation or system. Function concerns what a component, relationship or organized whole does, or what role it performs under particular conditions.

Structure and function are closely related, but they are not interchangeable. A description of how something is organized does not by itself establish what it does. Likewise, describing an observed function does not fully explain the structure that makes it possible.

Nor should every feature automatically be assumed to exist for a function. A function may be observed, experimentally demonstrated, inferred from a system's operation or attributed by an observer for a particular purpose. These different bases for functional descriptions should be made explicit.

Components and organization

A manifestation or system may contain components that are distinguishable by their properties, positions, relationships or roles. The components may be material, energetic, informational, organizational or of another kind, depending on the subject under investigation.

Organization concerns how components are arranged and related. The same components may form different arrangements, while different components may contribute to arrangements with similar functions. The significance of a component therefore cannot always be understood independently of its context and relationships. How such arrangements form connected wholes is also examined through Systems. A component may be identified as a component because it has a distinct boundary, because it performs a recognizable role, or simply because an observer finds it useful to isolate that part for analysis. Those criteria need not produce identical divisions.

Relationships within a structure

Structure is not necessarily a collection of isolated parts. Connections, dependencies, boundaries, positions and patterns of organization may be essential to how a whole is constituted and operates.

Research should examine which relationships are necessary for a particular structure, which are incidental, and how changes in those relationships affect the whole. A structure may also contain nested or overlapping arrangements that operate at different levels.

A diagram or classification can make an arrangement easier to understand, but the divisions shown in a representation should not be mistaken automatically for intrinsic divisions in the subject. Roles and functions

A component may perform one or more roles within a system. A role describes its contribution in a particular organizational context; a function describes an activity, operation or effect associated with that role.

The same component may have different functions in different systems, and a single function may be performed by different components or arrangements. Functional descriptions should therefore identify the system, conditions and level of analysis to which they apply.

It is also useful to distinguish what a component does from what it is used for. A use or purpose may be assigned by an external agent or observer, while an observed operation may occur without any demonstrated intention or purpose. Neither should be silently substituted for the other.

Structure-function relationships

Structure may enable, constrain or shape the functions that can occur. At the same time, ongoing functions and interactions may maintain, modify or reorganize a structure.

These relationships may involve feedback: an arrangement supports certain operations, while those operations contribute to preserving or changing the arrangement. Whether such feedback occurs, and how it operates, must be examined in each case rather than assumed universally.

A correlation between a particular structure and a particular function does not, by itself, establish the direction or mechanism of influence. The structure may enable the function, the function may help maintain the structure, both may depend on other conditions, or several relationships may operate together.

Levels and nested organization

Some manifestations and systems can be examined at multiple levels of organization. Components may form subsystems, subsystems may form larger systems, and a whole may itself be part of another organization. This nested organization is considered further in Scale & Levels. A function at one level may depend on structures and activities at another. A description that is useful at one level may not explain all properties at another, so research should identify the level at which a structural or functional claim is being made.

It should also avoid assuming that a function attributed to a whole can be assigned in the same way to each of its components, or that a component's function fully explains the behavior of the whole.

Structure, context and environment

The function of a structure may depend on its surroundings and on the systems with which it interacts. A component or arrangement may perform differently when conditions, inputs, constraints or relationships change.

Context is therefore part of a functional explanation. An apparent change in function may arise from a change in the structure itself, a change in its environment, a change in interacting systems, or a combination of these factors.

Stability, adaptation and reorganization

Some structures retain their organization across changing conditions, while others alter their arrangement or operation. A system may compensate for disturbances, adapt its organization, lose a function or develop a different one. These changes can be examined alongside Transformation & Emergence.

These possibilities raise questions about which features are maintained, which may change, and what conditions allow a structure to persist or reorganize. Stability should not be confused with the absence of change; some forms of stability may depend on ongoing activity and adjustment.

A change in function does not necessarily mean that the structure has changed, and a structural change does not necessarily mean that every function has changed. Each relationship needs to be investigated in context.

Different structures, similar functions

Similar functions may be achieved through different structures. Conversely, similar structures may perform different functions depending on their components, relationships, operating conditions or larger context.

Comparative research can investigate which features are shared, which differ, and whether the similarities reflect a common principle, a functional analogy or only a superficial resemblance. Similarity alone does not establish a common origin or mechanism.

Function, purpose and explanation

Functional explanations can be useful, but the statement that something performs a function does not automatically explain why the structure originated or how it came to exist. Current operation, historical development, assigned purpose and origination are distinct questions.

For example, identifying what a component contributes to a present system does not establish that the component originated specifically for that contribution. Such a conclusion would require additional evidence about the process by which the structure came about.

This distinction helps prevent an observed function from being treated as proof of intention, design or a particular origin. Those may be investigated as separate propositions.

Structure and function in newly emerging systems

When interactions give rise to a new system, its structure and functions may not be reducible to those of its contributing systems considered separately. New relationships and organizational arrangements may support functions that were not previously present in that combination.

Research can examine how such structures form, how their functions become identifiable, and whether the resulting system develops principles or capabilities of its own. These are open questions for investigation, not assumptions about every process of emergence.

Research directions

  • Developing clear descriptions of structure, organization, component, relationship, role and function.
  • Distinguishing observed functions, inferred functions, assigned uses and proposed purposes.
  • Identifying the components and relationships relevant to particular manifestations and systems.
  • Investigating how organization constrains, enables or shapes function.
  • Examining how functions maintain, modify or reorganize structures.
  • Testing the direction and mechanism of proposed structure-function relationships.
  • Comparing structural and functional descriptions across different levels of organization.
  • Investigating the effects of context, environment and interaction on function.
  • Distinguishing structural stability from the ongoing processes that may maintain it.
  • Comparing different structures that perform similar functions, and similar structures that perform different functions.
  • Separating functional explanations from claims about intention, design, historical development or origination.
  • Examining how new structures and functions arise through interactions and emergence.
  • Identifying which structural and functional properties can be observed, measured, tested or reproduced.
