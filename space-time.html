Space & Time

An exploration of what space and time are, whether they are distinct or interrelated, how they are described and measured, and whether their familiar forms apply to every manifestation or to Origin itself.

What do we mean by space?

Space is commonly described through extension, position, distance, direction and relationships between locations. These descriptions allow us to locate things and examine how they relate to one another. They do not, by themselves, settle what space fundamentally is.

Space might be understood as a fundamental aspect of existence, a condition within which manifestations occur, a relationship among manifestations, or a feature that arises within particular manifestations or systems. These are different conceptual possibilities, not interchangeable descriptions or established conclusions.

What do we mean by time?

Time is commonly described through sequence, duration, change and relationships between events. Clocks provide ways to measure intervals, while ordering events helps us describe processes. Neither measurement nor description alone answers what time itself is.

Research should distinguish time as measured by a particular clock or defined within a model, time as experienced by an observer, and the fundamental nature—if any—of time. These may be related, but should not be treated as identical without examination.

Conceptual divisions: space and time

People often discuss space and time as two separate categories. This distinction is useful for many purposes, but a distinction made in thought, language or measurement does not automatically demonstrate that reality is divided in the same way.

Conversely, describing space and time together as space-time does not, by itself, prove that every aspect of space and time is one indivisible thing. Separation, connection, unity and difference are questions to investigate—not conclusions to assume from the names or diagrams used to describe them.

Scientific models can treat concepts as distinct for analysis while also describing relationships between them. In modern physics, for example, relativity links spatial and temporal measurements within a space-time framework; measurements of distance, duration and simultaneity depend on the reference frame. This established feature of physical theory does not settle every broader question about the ultimate nature or scope of space and time.

Models, measurements and what they represent

Coordinates, rulers, clocks, reference frames and mathematical models are tools for describing and comparing observations. They should not be confused with the phenomena they represent. A coordinate boundary may be selected for convenience; a measured interval depends on the measurement procedure and its conditions.

Research should identify what is being measured, how the measurement is defined, which assumptions the model uses, and what the results actually support. A change in a description or coordinate system does not necessarily mean that the thing described has changed.

Space and time within manifestations

Space and time may be conditions through which manifestations exist and change. However, it should not be assumed in advance that every manifestation must share one spatial or temporal structure, or that the structures familiar from human experience are universal.

Different manifestations or systems might have different ways of relating position, distance, sequence and duration. They might share a framework, overlap only in part, or have relationships that current models do not adequately describe. These possibilities require clear definitions and evidence; unfamiliarity alone is neither proof nor disproof. The broader question of what it means for manifestations to exist is examined in Existence and Reality.

Origination and time

The relationship between origination and time raises a fundamental question: does origination require time, or is time a feature of processes within manifestations rather than a prerequisite for origination itself?

Within a manifestation, an event can be described as occurring before or after another event. Such descriptions depend on the temporal structure used to order those events. They do not, by themselves, establish that Origin or origination must be located within that same sequence.

It is therefore important to distinguish a first event within a particular system from Origin, and to distinguish temporal sequence within a manifestation from the question of how that manifestation originates. The idea of an idle state of Origin, explored in Zero — Idle State of Origin, also raises the question of whether familiar temporal descriptions apply before or beyond manifestation. This remains a conceptual research question, not an established physical finding.

Origin and space-time

Whether Origin exists within space and time, gives rise to them, or is not appropriately described by either concept remains open within this framework. Human experience and physical models provide important ways to investigate manifested conditions, but they should not automatically be treated as definitions of everything that may exist.

Space and time may be properties or conditions of manifestations rather than limits that can simply be assumed to apply to Origin. Other relationships are also possible. The framework does not decide among them in advance. See Origin for the foundational account of the source.

Different systems and possible interactions

If manifestations can operate according to different principles and rules, their spatial and temporal conditions may also differ. A difference between systems does not automatically mean that they are absolutely separate, nor does a possible interaction establish that their underlying structures are identical.

Research can ask what conditions allow systems to interact, whether an interaction requires a shared reference structure, and whether interactions can produce new spatial or temporal relationships. These questions connect with the study of Things, Energy and Matter, and Information and Patterns.

Beyond familiar space and time

Current human concepts and scientific models may not encompass every possible form of existence or manifestation. It remains open whether there are spatial, temporal or other kinds of relationships that differ fundamentally from those currently known or experienced.

This openness is not a claim that such forms have been found. It is a reason to avoid treating the limits of a current model, a measurement system or human intuition as automatically defining the limits of existence.

Research directions

  • Clarifying the meanings of space, time, position, distance, direction, sequence, duration and simultaneity.
  • Distinguishing observed phenomena from the concepts, coordinates and models used to describe them.
  • Examining which distinctions between space and time are useful analytical tools and which, if any, correspond to fundamental differences.
  • Investigating established physical relationships between spatial and temporal measurements, while identifying what those results do and do not establish.
  • Examining whether space and time are fundamental, relational, emergent or dependent on particular manifestations and systems.
  • Investigating whether different manifestations can have different spatial or temporal structures.
  • Distinguishing the temporal order of events within a system from the relationship between time, Origin and origination.
  • Exploring whether origination requires time or whether time describes conditions and processes within manifestations.
  • Examining how systems with different spatial or temporal structures might interact.
  • Identifying which questions can be observed, measured, compared or experimentally investigated, and which remain conceptual.
Research status: This page distinguishes established ways of describing and measuring space and time from broader conceptual possibilities within Science of Origin. It does not establish a particular ultimate theory, claim that all manifestations share one space-time structure, or claim that unfamiliar forms have been demonstrated. Definitions, methods, interpretations and evidence remain open to examination and revision.
