Scale & Levels

An exploration of the scales at which manifestations and systems can be examined, the levels at which they may be organized, and the relationships that connect processes and structures across different scales and levels.

Scale and level

Scale and level are related concepts, but they describe different aspects of investigation. Scale concerns the range, magnitude, size, duration or resolution at which something is considered. Level concerns a position, degree or layer of organization within a specified structure, process or system.

A change of scale changes the range or detail being examined. A change of level concerns how something is situated or organized in relation to other parts or layers. Neither term necessarily implies a fixed hierarchy, and neither should be assigned a universal meaning without identifying the subject and context.

Different scales of manifestation

Manifestations may be examined at different scales, from very small ranges to very large ones, and from brief processes to extended periods. The appropriate scale depends on the question being investigated and on the properties that can be observed or measured.

A description that is useful at one scale may omit details that become important at another. Conversely, details visible at a smaller scale may not by themselves explain patterns or behavior observed across a larger range. These differences do not necessarily indicate contradiction; they may reflect the distinct scope of each description.

Levels of organization

Some manifestations and systems can be described in terms of multiple levels of organization. A level may refer to components, arrangements of components, larger systems, or other distinguishable forms of organization. The relevant levels depend on the subject and the purpose of the investigation.

Levels should not automatically be treated as steps in a single ranking. They may describe different forms of organization, functional roles or perspectives. Research should state what makes a proposed level distinct and what relationships connect it to other levels.

Nested systems and containment

A system may contain subsystems, while itself forming part of a larger system. Such relationships can be described as nested or embedded arrangements. The boundaries and domains of each system may differ, and containment does not necessarily mean that every property or operating principle is shared.

A component may participate in more than one system, depending on which relationships and boundaries are being considered. Systems may therefore be nested, overlapping, connected or partially independent rather than arranged in one simple hierarchy.

Relationships across scales and levels

Processes and changes at one scale or level may influence what occurs at another. A local change may contribute to a wider pattern; conditions at a larger scale may shape the behavior of smaller components; and interactions among components may alter the organization of a larger system.

The direction, strength and nature of these relationships should be investigated in each case. Influence across scales should not be assumed merely because two events occur together, and a relationship observed in one system should not automatically be generalized to all systems.

Emergence across levels

Some properties may become identifiable only when components are considered together in a particular arrangement. Such properties are often described as emergent relative to the components or level from which the larger arrangement is examined.

Whether a property is genuinely emergent, merely unobserved at another scale, or explained by a different description is a question for investigation. Research should identify the property, the levels being compared, and the evidence for the proposed relationship.

Scale-dependent principles and rules

Different principles, rules or regularities may be relevant at different scales or levels of organization. A description that works within one defined range may not be sufficient outside that range. Some principles may remain applicable across several scales, while others may depend on particular conditions or forms of organization.

The scope of a principle should therefore be stated and tested rather than presumed to be universal or restricted. Apparent differences between levels may arise from different operating conditions, different system relationships, limits of observation, or principles that genuinely apply within different domains.

Observation and measurement

Observation and measurement are affected by the scale and level selected for investigation. Instruments, methods, units, resolution and sampling intervals determine which features can be detected and compared. A measurement may be meaningful at one scale while being unsuitable at another.

Researchers should identify the scale, level, resolution and boundaries used in a study. They should also consider whether the chosen method excludes relevant features, combines distinct processes, or introduces apparent divisions that are not intrinsic to the subject.

Transitions and changes of level

Systems may change their organization, combine, divide, expand, contract or develop new relationships. Such changes may alter the levels at which a system is usefully described. A transition between levels may involve a change in structure, function, behavior, boundaries or the relationships among components.

Not every change in description represents a physical transition. Research should distinguish a change in the system from a change in the observer's scale, classification or analytical perspective.

Research directions

  • Clarifying the meanings of scale, resolution, magnitude, level, layer, hierarchy and organization.
  • Identifying the relevant scales and levels for specific manifestations, processes and systems.
  • Investigating nested, embedded, overlapping and interconnected systems without assuming a universal hierarchy.
  • Examining how changes at one scale or level influence other scales or levels.
  • Studying properties that appear or become measurable when components form larger arrangements.
  • Testing the scope of principles and rules across different scales, levels and domains.
  • Examining how observation methods, instruments and measurement resolution affect reported findings.
  • Investigating transitions in organization and distinguishing them from changes in description or perspective.
  • Comparing whether proposed relationships remain consistent across different systems and contexts.
  • Determining which scale and level relationships can be observed, measured or experimentally investigated.
Research status: This page establishes working distinctions and research questions. It does not assume that all manifestations have the same levels, that systems form a single hierarchy, or that principles must change with scale. The nature and scope of particular scale and level relationships remain open to investigation and revision.
