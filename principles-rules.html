III · Structure

Principles and Rules

An exploration of how principles and rules operate within manifestations and systems, how different systems may coexist and interact, and how to distinguish conditions that belong to a system from concepts imposed by its observers.

Principles and rules within manifestations

In the developing Science of Origin framework, principles and rules are subjects for investigating how manifestations and the systems within them operate. Different manifestations may have different principles and rules. These need not be universal across all manifestations.

The terms principles and rules are related, but their precise meanings and relationship remain open to clarification. As a working distinction, a principle may describe a general basis or mode of operation, while a rule may describe a more specific condition, limitation or pattern governing what occurs within a defined context. This distinction is provisional and should be tested against actual cases.

Principles, rules and observed regularities

A recurring pattern of events may suggest that a system operates according to a principle or rule. However, an observed regularity, a proposed explanation and a confirmed relationship are not the same thing. Repetition alone does not establish why something occurs or whether the same relationship applies beyond the conditions observed.

Research should distinguish what is directly observed, what is inferred from observations, and what remains a working model. It should also identify the scope of a proposed principle or rule, the conditions under which it applies, and the evidence that could support or challenge it.

Discovered conditions and human-made conventions

Not every rule used to describe or organize the world necessarily operates as a rule within the thing being described. Some rules may be human-made conventions: definitions, classifications, measurement standards, legal arrangements or procedures created for particular purposes.

Such conventions can be useful and can have real effects when people or systems follow them. But the existence of a category or boundary in a human description does not, by itself, demonstrate that the same division exists as a fundamental separation in the subject. Conversely, the fact that a distinction is human-defined does not make it meaningless or useless.

Research should therefore ask whether a proposed division reflects an observed difference, a functional distinction, a chosen definition or an assumption. It should avoid confusing the map, classification or rule used by an observer with the structure or operating conditions of the manifestation itself. This connects with the investigation of Fabricated Boundaries and Simulated Separation.

Different levels and scopes of rules

A principle or rule may apply only within a particular system, process, scale, domain or set of conditions. A rule observed in one context should not automatically be generalized to a larger system or to every manifestation.

A single manifestation may contain multiple systems. Those systems may operate according to different principles or rules, including rules that appear incompatible with one another. The principles and rules observed in one system should not automatically be assumed to govern every other system within the same manifestation.

Different and apparently incompatible systems

Systems with different principles and rules may nevertheless coexist within one manifestation. Separate systems may also interact even when their operating principles or rules are not the same.

Apparent incompatibility should be examined in context. It may apply to particular operations, conditions or levels rather than to every possible interaction. Research can investigate whether the difference is genuinely irreconcilable, whether the systems operate in distinct domains, or whether an interaction is possible through conditions not yet understood.

These possibilities should not be collapsed into a single explanation. Coexistence does not prove that systems share identical rules, and difference does not by itself prove absolute separation.

Interaction, emergence and new systems

Interactions between systems may contribute to the emergence or origination of new systems. A resulting system may have principles and rules of its own, which may differ from those of the systems involved in its emergence.

New systems may therefore introduce arrangements and possibilities that were not present in any one contributing system considered separately. This is a research possibility within the developing framework, not an established empirical conclusion. The relationship between system interaction, emergence and origination requires further examination.

Changing rules and system development

Rules within a system may be stable, conditional, variable or subject to change. A change in an observed rule may reflect a change in the system, a change in its conditions, an interaction with another system, or a change in the observer's description. These possibilities should be distinguished rather than assumed to be equivalent.

A system may also develop new capabilities or arrangements under which different rules become relevant. A change in rules does not automatically mean that a new system has originated; nor does continuity of a familiar rule prove that the system itself has remained unchanged. These questions connect with Systems and Conditions and Constraints.

Independent origination capabilities

An emergent system may develop its own capability for origination. Its origination capability may differ from that of the system or systems from which it emerged.

Further systems may arise through these new capabilities. Their forms of origination may be fundamentally different from those of their originating systems, and they may interact with other systems to produce still-unfamiliar forms. These are open possibilities in the framework, not conclusions established by the present page.

Origin and the scope of possibility

The current conceptual position is that anything is allowed by the Ultimate Source, while expression and experience occur within manifestation(s). How this relationship should be understood, and what it implies for the principles and rules of particular systems, remain open questions.

Different principles or rules within manifestations need not be treated as limits on Origin itself. The distinction between the scope of Origin and the conditions operating within particular manifestations is an important subject for further examination. A rule belonging to a particular system should not be assumed to define the limits of Origin.

Research directions

  • Clarifying the meanings of principles, rules, regularities, conventions and models, and whether they represent distinct kinds of conditions.
  • Distinguishing directly observed regularities from explanations or rules inferred from them.
  • Investigating whether a proposed rule belongs to a system or is a human-made convention used to describe it.
  • Examining when human-defined categories correspond to meaningful differences and when they create misleading impressions of separation.
  • Identifying the scope and conditions under which particular principles and rules apply.
  • Investigating how principles and rules may differ between manifestations and between systems within one manifestation.
  • Examining how multiple systems with different or apparently incompatible rules can coexist and interact.
  • Exploring the conditions under which distinct systems can interact despite differences in their operating rules.
  • Studying how interactions may contribute to the emergence or origination of new systems.
  • Investigating whether emergent systems can develop their own origination capabilities and principles.
  • Distinguishing changes in a system's rules or conditions from transformation and the origination of a new system.
  • Examining the scope of Origin in relation to the principles and rules operating within manifestations.
Research status: This page records developing conceptual propositions, working distinctions and research questions. The proposed relationships are open to clarification, examination and revision; they are not presented as independently established scientific findings. Human-made conventions, observed regularities and system-level rules must be distinguished in each investigation rather than presumed to be equivalent.
