From questions to findings

Research and Discoveries

A practical structure for investigating questions, recording observations, evaluating evidence and documenting what research does—and does not—establish.

Purpose

This page describes how research within Science of Origin can be organized and assessed. The Research Framework presents the overall scope and conceptual organization of the project; this page focuses on the progress of individual investigations and the status of their results.

The aim is to remain open to new possibilities while distinguishing clearly between ideas, observations, evidence, interpretations and conclusions.

1. Research questions

Each investigation begins with a clearly stated question. A useful research question identifies what is being examined, the scope of the inquiry and, where possible, what kind of observation or result could help address it.

Questions may be conceptual, observational, comparative or experimental. Not every question about Origin can necessarily be tested with current methods, and a question should not be presented as experimentally testable until a meaningful method has been identified.

2. Research methods

The method should be appropriate to the question. Depending on the subject, an investigation may involve defining terms, developing a model, making observations, comparing cases, taking measurements or conducting an experiment.

Where measurement or experimentation is applicable, the method should be described clearly enough for its limitations to be understood and, where feasible, for others to repeat it. A method should not be treated as proof merely because it produces a result.

3. Observations and records

Observations should be recorded separately from explanations or interpretations. A useful record states what was observed, under what conditions, by what method and with what limitations.

Unexpected results and observations that do not fit an existing idea should be retained rather than omitted. The record should make it possible to distinguish direct observations from recollections, assumptions and later interpretations.

4. Evidence and evaluation

Evidence is evaluated in relation to the question and the method that produced it. Relevant considerations may include the quality and completeness of the record, consistency across observations, alternative explanations, measurement limitations and whether the result can be independently checked.

One observation may motivate further investigation without being sufficient to establish a general conclusion. Repeated or reproducible results can strengthen support for a claim, but their significance still depends on the quality of the method and the scope of the evidence.

5. Verification and reproducibility

Where feasible, methods and results should be checked through repetition, independent examination or a different method addressing the same question. Differences between attempts should be documented and investigated rather than hidden.

Some subjects may not currently allow controlled experiments or straightforward replication. In those cases, the limits of verification should be stated explicitly; the absence of a suitable test is not itself confirmation or disproof.

6. Discoveries and conclusions

A discovery is a finding that emerges from investigation and is supported by an adequate body of evidence for the claim being made. The wording of a conclusion should not extend beyond what the observations and methods support.

Findings may be preliminary, limited to particular conditions or in need of independent verification. Conclusions should remain open to revision when new evidence, better methods or more complete explanations become available.

7. Unresolved questions

Every investigation may leave questions unanswered. These should be recorded as part of the research outcome, together with uncertainties, limitations and possible next steps.

Unknowns are not failures to be concealed. Identifying what remains unknown helps define the next useful investigation and prevents an incomplete explanation from being mistaken for a final answer.

Research record

Individual investigations can use a consistent record so that their progress and status remain understandable over time. A record may include:

  • Research question: the specific question being investigated.
  • Scope and definitions: what the investigation includes and how key terms are used.
  • Method: how observations or results are obtained.
  • Observations: what was recorded, without adding unsupported interpretation.
  • Evidence: the information relevant to assessing the question.
  • Evaluation: limitations, alternative explanations and verification status.
  • Current conclusion: what the available evidence supports at this stage.
  • Open questions: what remains unresolved and what could be investigated next.

Research status distinctions

  • Concept: an idea or proposed way of understanding a subject.
  • Possibility: an option that remains open but has not been established.
  • Observation: something recorded through an identified process or method.
  • Interpretation: an explanation or meaning assigned to observations.
  • Preliminary finding: a result with some supporting evidence but important limitations or outstanding checks.
  • Supported finding: a conclusion supported by evidence appropriate to its stated scope and method.
  • Unresolved: a question for which the available investigation does not yet support a conclusion.

These labels describe the status of a claim, not its importance. A possibility may be worth investigating, and an unresolved question may be central to the project.

Research status: This page establishes a proposed working approach for documenting research. It does not claim that the questions or conceptual positions elsewhere on this site have already been experimentally verified or established as scientific discoveries.
