States & Processes

An exploration of the conditions in which things exist and the processes through which they operate, persist, develop and change.

States and processes

A state describes the condition or configuration of something at a particular time or within a defined interval. A process describes an activity, sequence or set of changes through which something operates or develops. The distinction helps separate what is observed at a given point from what occurs across time.

States and processes are related but not interchangeable. A state may be the result of earlier processes, while ongoing processes may maintain, modify or replace a state.

Describing a state

A state may be described through the properties, conditions, arrangement and relationships that characterize a thing or system. Its description depends on which properties are relevant, how they are observed and the level of detail required for the investigation.

A state is not necessarily static in an absolute sense. It may remain sufficiently stable for a particular purpose while smaller or slower changes continue beyond the resolution of observation.

Describing a process

A process involves activity or change over time. It may include a sequence of events, continuous activity, exchanges, transformations or coordinated operations. Processes may be regular or irregular, gradual or abrupt, continuous or intermittent.

Describing a process requires attention to its duration, stages, conditions, participants and observable changes. A process may be identified by its activity, its effects, or both, without assuming that every process has a single simple cause.

State, stability and persistence

Some states persist over time despite disturbances or ongoing activity. Persistence may result from stable conditions, continuing processes, compensating changes or relationships that maintain a recognizable configuration.

Stability is relative to the properties, timescale and measurement resolution being considered. A system may be stable in one respect and changing in another. Research should specify what is stable, over what interval and under which conditions.

Change and transitions between states

A process may move a thing or system from one state to another. A transition may be gradual, abrupt, reversible, irreversible or dependent on particular conditions. Some transitions are readily observable; others may involve intermediate states that are difficult to distinguish.

Not every change produces a clearly separate state. The identification of states and transitions depends partly on how the subject is divided and described for research.

Continuous and discrete descriptions

Some processes are described as continuous, with properties changing through an uninterrupted range. Others are described as discrete, with identifiable steps, events or state changes. A single phenomenon may support both descriptions at different scales or for different research purposes.

Choosing a continuous or discrete model should reflect the evidence and the intended description. A useful model may simplify a process, but its categories should not be mistaken for features that necessarily exist in the subject itself.

Processes within systems

Systems may contain multiple processes operating at the same time. These processes may be independent, coordinated, competing or interdependent. Some processes maintain the system's current organization, while others contribute to its development or transformation.

Processes may also cross system boundaries or connect several systems. Their effects may depend on interactions among processes, system conditions and the principles or rules applicable within each system.

Multiple timescales

Processes may operate over very different durations. Rapid events may occur within slower processes, and short-term states may form part of longer patterns of persistence or change. Observations made over one interval may therefore reveal only part of a wider process.

Comparing timescales can help distinguish immediate changes from long-term development, recurring activity or gradual shifts in system organization.

Feedback and process relationships

Processes may affect the conditions under which other processes operate. In feedback arrangements, the results of a process may influence its later activity or the activity of another process. Such relationships may reinforce, regulate or otherwise alter ongoing change.

Feedback is one possible relationship among processes; it should be identified through evidence about the relevant connections and effects rather than inferred from repetition or correlation alone.

Observation and representation

States are often represented through measurements or descriptions taken at particular times. Processes may be represented through sequences, timelines, rates, trajectories or records of events. Combining these representations can help relate observed conditions to the changes occurring between observations.

Observation intervals, measurement limits and the choice of variables can affect whether a process appears continuous, intermittent, stable or changing. Research should make these choices explicit.

Research directions

  • Clarifying the distinction between a state, a condition, an event, an activity and a process.
  • Identifying which properties are needed to describe a state in a given investigation.
  • Examining how states persist while processes and smaller changes continue.
  • Investigating gradual, abrupt, reversible and irreversible transitions between states.
  • Comparing continuous and discrete descriptions of the same process.
  • Identifying multiple processes operating within a system and examining their relationships.
  • Studying processes that cross boundaries or connect different systems.
  • Examining how processes operate across different timescales.
  • Investigating feedback among processes and its effects on persistence, regulation and change.
  • Determining how observation intervals and measurement choices affect descriptions of states and processes.
Research status: This page establishes working distinctions and research directions. It does not assume that every observed state is static, that every process has a single cause, or that one description of states and processes applies to all manifestations and systems. Definitions, mechanisms and evidence remain open to examination and revision.
