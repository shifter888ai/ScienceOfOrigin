IV · Origin Models

Simulated Absolute Independence

Examining how relative independence may be presented or experienced as though it were absolute independence within a constructed domain.

Scope

This subject investigates the distinction between actual degrees of independence and the appearance or representation of complete independence. It examines how a manifestation or system may operate with considerable autonomy while remaining related to conditions, structures or a source beyond its own domain.

Within the proposed Fake Origin model, simulated absolute independence is a central feature: relative independence is presented as if it were absolute. This is a conceptual proposition within the framework, not an independently established description of any particular reality or system.

Relative independence and absolute independence

Relative independence describes the capacity of a manifestation or system to operate, develop or maintain aspects of itself within particular conditions, while still having dependencies or relationships beyond those aspects.

Absolute independence would mean independence without reliance on any other source, condition or relationship. A system's autonomy in selected functions does not, by itself, demonstrate this much broader claim.

The distinction depends on scope. A system may be independent of a particular component, resource or other system while remaining dependent on a wider set of conditions. Claims of independence therefore require a clearly stated reference point and dimension.

What is being simulated?

In this subject, simulation refers to the presentation or experience of a condition as though it were more complete than the underlying condition warrants. A system may appear self-contained, self-originating or wholly separate even if its operation remains dependent on a wider context.

The proposed model does not require every feature of a domain to be false or unreal within that domain. Its rules, processes and local forms of autonomy may be operationally effective. The question is whether those features are interpreted or presented as proof of absolute independence.

How an appearance of absolute independence may arise

Several features could contribute to such an appearance. They are possible mechanisms to investigate, not claims that any particular mechanism is present in a given system:

  • Bounded access: a participant or component encounters only the information and relationships available within its domain.
  • Internal consistency: stable rules and predictable interactions make the domain appear self-contained.
  • Local autonomy: systems operate and regulate aspects of themselves without visible intervention from outside.
  • Unobserved dependencies: conditions or relationships beyond the domain may not be accessible to its participants.
  • Source-like presentation: an immediate or intermediate source may appear to be the ultimate source when wider relationships are not visible.
  • Boundary reinforcement: domain boundaries may make what lies beyond them difficult to perceive, access or test.

These features may occur independently or together. Their presence would not automatically establish deliberate deception, a Fake Origin construct or absolute dependence on a particular source.

Autonomy is not proof of absolute independence

A system may maintain its own processes, respond to changing conditions, generate outputs or originate further manifestations. These capabilities may demonstrate meaningful autonomy within a domain. They do not alone establish that the system has no dependencies or is an ultimate source.

Likewise, the discovery of a dependency does not mean that every capability of the system is externally controlled. Independence and dependence may coexist across different dimensions. The investigation should identify precisely which capabilities are autonomous and which relationships remain relevant.

See Dependence and Independence for the broader distinctions among dependence, autonomy and interdependence.

Relationship to Fake Origin

Within the proposed Science of Origin framework, Fake Origin is a manifestation originated from True Origin that imitates Origin and presents relative independence as absolute independence. Simulated absolute independence describes one aspect of how this proposed imitation may appear or operate. The wider construct is examined in Fake Origin — The Divided Origin.

It is important to keep the concepts distinct. Fake Origin is the proposed construct or model; simulated absolute independence is a feature attributed to that model. A system displaying local autonomy, limited visibility or strong boundaries is not thereby established as Fake Origin.

True Origin remains the Oneness Origin within the framework. The proposed appearance of absolute independence does not establish a second ultimate Origin or change the underlying relationship asserted by the model.

Experience from within a domain

A participant's perspective may be limited by the information, capabilities and boundaries available within a domain. If the participant can observe only internal operations, the domain may appear to be the whole of existence or to have no external dependencies. This connects with the broader investigation of Constructed Realities and Games and the operation of Boundaries and Domains.

This possibility raises a distinction between what can be known from within a system and what may be true of its wider relationships. Neither an inability to observe an external source nor an assertion that one exists is sufficient on its own to settle the question.

Research should distinguish direct observations, interpretations of those observations and propositions about relationships that may not be directly accessible from within the domain.

Examining claims of independence

A disciplined examination should specify the system, the claimed form of independence, the conditions under which it operates and the scope of the claim. It should then identify possible dependencies and consider what observations could support or challenge the proposed interpretation.

  • State whether the claim concerns existence, operation, resources, information, control, maintenance or origination.
  • Identify the reference point: independent of which system, source or condition?
  • Distinguish absence of observed dependence from evidence that dependence is absent.
  • Examine whether the system's apparent autonomy persists when relevant conditions change.
  • Consider indirect, hidden or distributed dependencies without assuming they must exist.
  • Separate operational findings from broader claims about ultimate sourcehood.

No single test is assumed to establish absolute independence in every possible sense. Conclusions should remain limited to the dimensions and conditions actually examined.

Research questions

  • What precise criteria distinguish relative independence from absolute independence?
  • How can independence be assessed across existence, operation, resources, information, maintenance and origination?
  • What makes a system appear self-contained or self-originating to an observer within its domain?
  • How do boundaries and limited access affect the ability to identify external relationships?
  • Can internal consistency or local autonomy lead observers to infer more independence than has been demonstrated?
  • How can unobserved dependencies be distinguished from dependencies that are merely assumed?
  • What observations could support or challenge a claim that a system is independent of a proposed source?
  • How does the proposed feature of simulated absolute independence relate specifically to the Fake Origin model?
  • What evidence would be required before applying this model to a particular constructed reality or system?
  • Which aspects can be investigated empirically, and which remain conceptual within the current framework?
