II · Nature of Existence

Nature of Life

Investigating what Life is, what distinguishes a Life Form, and how Life may relate to Things, Consciousness, Freewill and Origination.

Foundational principle

All things are lifeforms, without exception. Not knowing this is one of the biggest misunderstandings of humanity.

Life is therefore not restricted to organisms conventionally recognized as biological. All Things are Life Forms, although their characteristics, organization, activity and modes of manifestation may differ. The conventional living/non-living distinction is a classification, not an absolute division of existence.

All existences also have Consciousness and are Consciousness, without exception. The relationship between Life and Consciousness is therefore foundational; research investigates how they manifest and operate across different forms.

Life as a subject of existence

Life is a fundamental subject within the Nature of Existence. Its familiar examples provide important starting points for investigation, but they should not automatically be treated as defining every possible form of Life.

Science of Origin approaches Life as a subject whose nature, scope and distinguishing properties require careful examination. A useful description should identify the characteristics being considered without assuming that one feature or familiar example settles the question universally.

Forms and characteristics of Life

Since all Things are Life Forms, research examines the different forms and characteristics through which Life manifests. Possible dimensions for investigation include organization, activity, continuity, development, interaction, self-maintenance and the capacity to respond to conditions.

These dimensions are research considerations, not a proposed universal definition. Their relevance may differ across forms, and any criteria should be stated clearly enough to be examined and compared.

Living and non-living classifications

The conventional distinction between living and non-living Things can describe selected observable characteristics, but it does not establish a fundamental division between Life and non-Life. All Things are Life Forms, without exception.

Research should examine what the conventional classifications describe, why they are used, and where they may be insufficient to characterize the full range of Life Forms. Practical categories should not be mistaken for ultimate boundaries in existence.

Life and Things

Life Forms are also Things within the broad use of that term, but the properties and processes associated with Life may require distinct investigation. The relationship between the general category of Things and the particular characteristics of Life should therefore remain explicit.

See Things & Life for the broader discussion of Things, Life Forms and their relationship to manifestation.

Life and Consciousness

All existences have Consciousness and are Consciousness, without exception. Accordingly, every Life Form involves Consciousness. Research examines how Consciousness is expressed, experienced, structured and manifested across different forms of Life.

Human experience may provide a point of reference, but should not be assumed to define the full range of possible forms or expressions of Consciousness. The framework's foundational position is discussed in Consciousness and Freewill.

Life and Freewill

How do Life, Freewill, intention and choice relate? Do different Life Forms have different capacities to form intentions, make choices or realize intentions? What can be observed about these capacities, and what would distinguish an interpretation from evidence?

Within the Science of Origin framework, Consciousness and Freewill are treated as closely connected foundational concepts. Their relationship to particular Life Forms and their capabilities remains a subject for investigation rather than an assumed conclusion. See Consciousness and Freewill and Origination for the related foundational subjects.

Life, development and continuity

Life may involve processes of maintenance, growth, development, adaptation, reproduction or transformation. These processes should be examined separately: the presence of one does not necessarily establish the presence of all the others, nor does a change in a Life Form automatically constitute the origination of a new one.

Research can investigate what persists through change, what conditions support continuity, and how new forms or capabilities may arise. The scope of each claim should match the observations and methods used.

Different and unfamiliar forms of Life

Known forms of Life should not be assumed to exhaust every possibility. Life may, as an open research possibility, take forms that differ substantially from familiar biological examples or operate under different conditions.

Openness to unfamiliar possibilities does not establish that a proposed form exists. Any candidate should be described clearly, characterized using stated criteria, and examined through appropriate observations or methods.

Origin and Origination of Life

How do Life Forms come into manifestation? This question connects the nature of Life with Origin and Origination, while keeping the ultimate source distinct from the processes through which particular manifestations arise.

Research may examine whether different Life Forms arise through different pathways, whether origination can occur within existing manifestations, and whether interactions among systems contribute to new forms. These questions should not be collapsed into a single assumed mechanism.

Research directions

  • Clarifying the scope and criteria of Life and Life Form.
  • Investigating the different forms, characteristics and manifestations of Life across all Things.
  • Examining the conventional living/non-living classification and the limits of its descriptive usefulness.
  • Investigating how Consciousness is expressed and manifested across different forms of Life.
  • Examining how Life may relate to Freewill, intention, choice and the realization of intentions.
  • Studying maintenance, development, adaptation, reproduction, continuity and transformation as distinct processes.
  • Comparing known and proposed unfamiliar forms of Life without assuming that familiar examples define all possibilities.
  • Investigating the origination of Life Forms and the role of interactions among manifestations and systems.
  • Developing observation and measurement methods appropriate to the properties under investigation.
  • Distinguishing observed properties and reproducible findings from interpretations and unresolved possibilities.
