# Central Research History — Science of Origin
## Architecture and Working Principles

**Status:** Working architecture for review  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Prepared:** November 20, 2026  
**Canonical website:** https://scienceoforigin.org/  
**Scope:** All Science of Origin research, across every division, field, application and future research direction.

---

## 1. Purpose

The Central Research History is the shared historical foundation of Science of Origin. It preserves the development of research across all subjects and fields, while making it possible to discover relationships among records created at different times or in different research contexts.

Its scope includes Zero-Origin Research, Science of Consciousness, Spectrum research, research into the Nature of Life and the Nature of Things, methodological exploration, applications, and future research directions. It is not an archive belonging to any single field.

The archive serves both historical preservation and future research. It should help readers understand what was recorded, how understanding developed, what later changed, and what new connections may still be discovered.

**Guiding statement:**

> Preserve the past. Explore the present. Keep the future discoverable.

A complementary principle is:

> The past is not finished teaching us.

A historical record may reveal new meaning years after it was first created. Its research value can grow as experience accumulates, other records become available, and previously unrecognized relationships are discovered.

## 2. Three complementary layers

These are different presentation and source levels within one continuous research undertaking, not separate bodies of knowledge.

### 2.1 Research pages — current and evolving understanding

Research pages present the current understanding of a subject, field, method or application. They may include current scope and objectives, concepts and terminology, methods, observations, experiences, findings, open questions, and links to relevant historical records and original sources.

Research pages may be updated as understanding develops. They are not substitutes for the historical record.

### 2.2 Central Research History — chronological development

The Central Research History preserves dated research developments across all Science of Origin fields. Records may document discussions and question-and-answer exchanges, observations and reported experiences, research questions and propositions, discoveries, conceptual developments, methodological changes, interpretations, reassessments and corrections.

A record is preserved once as a shared historical source. It may connect to many research fields and subjects without being copied into separate field-specific histories.

### 2.3 Original sources — traceable source material

Where available and appropriate for publication, original sources may include discussions, conversations, source documents, notes, dated observations, experience records, measurements, experimental records and supporting materials.

A structured historical record or summary is not the same as its original source. The archive should identify the source basis and link to original material when available and suitable to share.

Personal or sensitive information, including students' identities and private communications, must not be published without appropriate permission and review.

## 3. One record, multiple research connections

A historical record is not restricted to the field in which its subject first appeared. One discussion or experience may be relevant to several fields. A later discovery may reveal a connection that was not recognized when the record was first created.

**One historical record, multiple research connections.**

For example, a record concerning Zero-Origin Research may later be relevant to Science of Consciousness, Spectrum research, intelligence, experience or another field. These are possible connections to examine, not automatic claims that the fields or concepts are equivalent.

The record remains in one chronological archive. Research pages and indexes link to it from their respective perspectives.

## 4. Historical record structure

Each published record should use a consistent structure. Not every field will be available for every record; unavailable information should be identified as unknown or not yet recovered rather than invented.

### 4.1 Core identity and chronology

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Record ID | Permanent unique identifier for the archive record |
| Title | Clear description of the record |
| Research date or period | When the original research, discussion or experience took place, if known |
| Date basis | Exact, approximate, inferred from a source, or unknown |
| Archive / publication date | When the record was added to or published in the archive |
| Record type | Discussion, observation, experience, discovery, method, reassessment, source collection, or another appropriate type |
| Record status | Original-source transcript, recovered source, structured summary, or later editorial account |

The research date and archive/publication date must remain separate. A record recovered or published today may describe research from many years earlier.

Record IDs must remain stable and must not imply an unverified research chronology. An administrative number is not proof of the date or sequence of the original research.

### 4.2 Research content and context

Where relevant, preserve:

- **Original context:** What prompted the discussion, observation or investigation.
- **Original question or subject:** What was being examined.
- **Original statements and observations:** What the source actually records.
- **Research development:** How the discussion or investigation proceeded.
- **Understanding at the time:** What was understood or concluded at that stage.
- **Open questions:** What remained unresolved or unexplored.
- **Outcome or next direction:** What followed, if documented.

Distinguish what was actually said or observed from later summaries, interpretations and editorial explanations.

### 4.3 Research connections

Each record may connect to multiple Science of Origin divisions, research fields and applications; subjects, concepts and terms; other historical records; current research pages; and original sources.

Connections should be descriptive and expandable. A primary placement may help navigation, but must not imply exclusive relevance.

### 4.4 Source and provenance

For every record, identify as much as can be supported: original source or location; source date or period; whether the source is complete, partial, recovered or unavailable; whether the published record is a transcript, extract, summary or editorial reconstruction; and uncertainty about wording, chronology or context.

Do not present reconstructed wording as an exact quotation. Do not silently fill source gaps with later knowledge.

## 5. Cross-record discovery

Cross-record discovery is a primary function of the archive, not merely a search convenience.

Two or more records may reveal something that none reveals independently. Their relationship may become visible only after other experiences, records or research developments accumulate.

The archive should support:

1. **Chronological retrieval** — locate records by research date, period or archive sequence.
2. **Thematic retrieval** — locate records by subject, concept, phenomenon or terminology.
3. **Cross-field retrieval** — locate records connected to multiple divisions, fields or applications.
4. **Relationship discovery** — identify possible relationships among records, including records created independently or years apart.
5. **Evolution tracking** — follow how a concept, question, observation or interpretation develops across records.
6. **Retrospective discovery** — add newly recognized relationships to older records without changing their original content.

### 5.1 Connection status

Connections should have a clear status so a possible relationship is not mistaken for an established one:

- **Proposed connection:** A possible relationship has been noticed and recorded.
- **Under examination:** The relationship is being compared or investigated.
- **Supported connection:** Further research provides a stated basis for retaining the relationship.
- **Revised connection:** The relationship or its interpretation has changed.
- **Not supported / set aside:** Examination did not support the proposed relationship, or it is no longer being pursued.

A connection's status should be traceable to the relevant research or reassessment record. A link alone does not establish causation, equivalence, dependency or proof.

### 5.2 Connections can create new research

A connection may reveal an implication not recognized in either record alone, a relationship between previously separate fields, a new question or research direction, a reason to revisit an earlier interpretation, or a new concept, method or possible discovery.

These developments should become new records linked to the records that contributed to them.

## 6. Interpretive evolution and historical integrity

A record's original content and chronology must remain identifiable. Its meaning or research significance may be reconsidered as understanding develops.

**Historical records remain stable; interpretations remain open.**

A later discovery may reveal a different implication, relationship, significance or interpretation of an earlier record. The original record must not be silently rewritten to make it appear that the later understanding was already present.

### 6.1 Interpretation history

Where an interpretation changes materially, preserve a traceable sequence:

1. **Original understanding:** What was understood at the time of the original record.
2. **New experience or connection:** What later experience, record or discovery prompted reconsideration.
3. **Reassessment:** What was reconsidered and why.
4. **Revised interpretation:** The later understanding, clearly dated and attributed to its source.
5. **Further implications:** New questions, connections or research directions that follow.

A later interpretation may be attached as a dated annotation or documented in a new reassessment record. The original source and earlier interpretation remain accessible.

### 6.2 No record is intellectually exhausted

No historical record should be treated as permanently exhausted of meaning or future research value. This does not mean every old record must continually be reinterpreted; it means the archive must not prevent future examination, connection or reassessment.

## 7. Temporal research perspective

Experiences and observations across different time scales may be very different. A short-term appearance may change through mid-term development or long-term experience. An initial observation may be genuine while its meaning, cause, significance or apparent consistency is misunderstood.

The archive should preserve temporal perspective whenever relevant.

### 7.1 Three temporal stages

- **Short-term experience:** What is observed or experienced in the immediate or shorter period.
- **Mid-term experience:** How the subject, pattern, response or interaction develops over an intermediate period.
- **Long-term experience:** What becomes visible through extended experience, persistence, change, delayed effects or later comparison.

The exact duration of these stages may depend on the subject. This architecture does not impose universal numerical boundaries where none have been established.

### 7.2 Compare, do not collapse

When records permit, compare short-term, mid-term and long-term observations rather than treating them as interchangeable.

A short-term observation may later be reinforced, qualified, reinterpreted, shown to be temporary or context-dependent, contradicted by later experience, or connected to a pattern not visible at first.

The original observation remains preserved even when its later interpretation changes.

### 7.3 Temporal reliability and continuous reassessment

Understanding examined across multiple time scales has undergone a different depth of examination from understanding based only on a short-term observation. This can provide stronger experiential support, but duration alone does not prove correctness.

Preserve the actual time span, experiences, comparisons and reasons for reassessment rather than treating age as automatic validation.

The researcher's practical standard of drawing on at least ten years of accumulated experience and records before answering students on certain matters is an important reported research practice. It is that researcher's standard, not a universal ten-year scientific rule.

**Working principle:**

> Time will tell. Do not judge an understanding solely by what it reveals today. Allow time, experience and historical comparison to reveal what it may mean.

## 8. Evidence, interpretation and research status

The archive must preserve distinctions among different kinds of content. Depending on the source, a record may contain a directly recorded statement; a reported experience or observation; a measurement or experimental result; an interpretation of an experience; a research proposition or open question; a later editorial summary; or an independently supported finding.

These categories must not be silently merged. A reported experience should not automatically be presented as independently demonstrated; an interpretation should not be mistaken for the original observation; and a research objective should not be presented as an established conclusion.

Where the basis is uncertain or incomplete, say so plainly. The archive should support examination, not manufacture certainty.

## 9. Corrections, additions and versions

Historical integrity requires a traceable correction process:

- Do not silently overwrite original historical content.
- Correct clear transcription or metadata errors through dated, documented amendments.
- Preserve earlier wording or versions when a correction changes meaning.
- Add later interpretations as dated annotations or new records.
- Link a reassessment to the original record and to records that prompted it.
- Keep a record's stable ID when its title, tags or explanatory notes are improved.
- If a record must be split, preserve links showing its relationship to the original.

The goal is not to prevent correction. It is to make correction and development visible.

## 10. Relationship with current research pages

Research pages organize current understanding by subject, field or application. The Central Research History organizes historical development across all subjects and fields. Original sources provide the traceable basis for historical accounts wherever available.

A current research page should link to relevant historical records. A historical record should link to current research pages where its subject is presently discussed.

When a new connection changes current understanding, update the relevant research page as appropriate and document the interpretive development in the historical record. Do not revise the past to imitate the present.

## 11. Future discovery capabilities

The architecture should support increasingly rich discovery without requiring a particular technical implementation immediately.

Possible future capabilities include:

- searching by concept, phrase, date, source and research field;
- filtering by temporal stage and record status;
- displaying records connected to a selected record;
- tracing how an interpretation changed over time;
- finding concepts that recur across different fields;
- identifying possible relationships among records not previously connected;
- showing how a later discovery draws on earlier records;
- generating cross-field views without duplicating source records.

Any automatically suggested relationship must be presented as a candidate for human examination, not as a verified discovery merely because a system identified it.

The archive itself may become a research instrument: the combination of records can reveal questions and relationships that were not visible in any one record.

## 12. Implementation principles

The initial implementation should remain simple, transparent and reversible.

1. Retain and build on the existing Central Research History page as the public entry point.
2. Establish a consistent record template and stable record identifiers.
3. Start by indexing and connecting already published records.
4. Add recovered historical material with clear source and chronology qualifications.
5. Connect records to multiple research fields without duplication.
6. Add dated interpretive annotations and reassessment records as new understanding develops.
7. Expand search and relationship-discovery capabilities incrementally.
8. Keep the archive usable as a human-readable website before advanced search or automated discovery is available.

The Central Research History belongs on the canonical Science of Origin website:

**https://scienceoforigin.org/**

The .org domain should be used consistently in public-facing canonical references. Existing repository references to other domains should be reviewed separately and corrected only through an explicit, carefully scoped change.

## 13. Guiding principles

1. **One continuous research undertaking:** The archive covers all Science of Origin research.
2. **One record, multiple research connections:** Preserve a source record once and connect it across fields.
3. **Chronology with provenance:** Dates and sequence must be supported and their basis made clear.
4. **Original sources remain identifiable:** A summary is not an original source.
5. **Cross-record discovery:** Relationships among records may reveal discoveries unavailable from individual records.
6. **Historical integrity:** Original records are not silently rewritten.
7. **Interpretive evolution:** New experiences and connections may change how old records are understood.
8. **Temporal perspective:** Short-term, mid-term and long-term experience may reveal different things.
9. **Continuous reassessment:** Time and accumulated experience can expose mistaken or incomplete interpretations.
10. **No intellectual exhaustion:** Old records remain open to future examination and discovery.
11. **Evidence and interpretation remain distinguishable:** The archive does not turn propositions or reports into established conclusions.
12. **Open-ended expandability:** New fields, methods, relationships and forms of discovery can be incorporated without making the current architecture a boundary around future research.

## 14. Current status and next steps

This document defines the proposed architecture for review. It does not claim that the full archive, metadata system, cross-record graph or automated discovery capabilities have already been implemented.

Recommended next steps:

1. Review and approve this architecture.
2. Refine the historical record template using a small set of existing records from different Science of Origin fields.
3. Test whether the template preserves chronology, provenance, temporal perspective and cross-field relationships without unnecessary duplication.
4. Revise the existing Central Research History index to reflect the approved structure.
5. Begin gradual indexing and connection of the wider historical record.
6. Develop advanced cross-record discovery only after the underlying records and relationships are sufficiently organized.

**Preserve the past. Explore the present. Keep the future discoverable.**
