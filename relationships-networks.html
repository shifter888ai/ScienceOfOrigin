III · Structure

Relationships & Networks

An exploration of the connections and dependencies among components, systems and manifestations, and how multiple relationships form networks that may shape organization, interaction and origination.

Relationships and connections

A relationship describes a connection, association or dependence between two or more things, properties, events, systems or manifestations. A relationship may be direct or indirect, temporary or persistent, strong or weak, and may take different forms depending on the context in which it is examined.

Identifying that two things are related does not by itself explain the nature, direction, cause or significance of their relationship. These aspects require separate investigation.

Relationships and interactions

A relationship describes how things are connected or stand in relation to one another. An interaction concerns a process through which connected things act upon, exchange with or influence one another. A relationship may exist without an observable interaction at a particular moment, while an interaction may create, alter or end relationships.

Keeping these concepts distinct can help clarify whether research is examining a connection, an ongoing process, or the effects produced through that process. This distinction is developed further in Interactions.

Types and directions of relationships

Relationships may be classified in different ways. They may be one-way or reciprocal, equal or unequal, dependent or independent, close or distant, direct or mediated. Some relationships may be mutual, while others may be asymmetric or involve different roles on each side.

A relationship may also connect many participants rather than only two. Its description should specify the participants, the properties being related and the conditions under which the relationship applies.

Dependency and interdependence

One thing may depend on another for its existence, operation, development or particular properties. Dependencies may be necessary, conditional, partial or context-specific. Two or more things may also depend on one another in different ways, forming interdependent arrangements.

Dependence should not automatically be interpreted as causation. A shared condition may account for an observed association, and the direction or mechanism of a dependency may require further evidence. Related questions of autonomy and mutual reliance are examined in Dependence & Independence, while cause and effect are considered in Causality & Influence.

Networks and their elements

A network is a set of elements connected through relationships. The elements may be things, components, systems, events or other identifiable units; the connections may represent interactions, dependencies, exchanges, similarities or another defined relationship.

In a network description, the elements are often represented as nodes and the relationships as links or edges. This is a useful representation, but the choice of nodes and connections depends on the research question. Different representations of the same subject may reveal different aspects of its organization. The criteria for identifying a system and its components are explored in Systems.

Network structure and organization

Networks may differ in how many connections their elements have, how those connections are distributed, whether they form clusters, and whether some elements occupy especially connected or central positions. A network may be sparse or dense, centralized or distributed, hierarchical or non-hierarchical.

These descriptions characterize particular network structures; they do not by themselves establish how a network operates, why it formed or what effects it produces. Structure and function should be investigated separately and in relation to one another, as explored in Structure & Function. Measures of order and complexity provide another perspective on network organization; see Order & Complexity.

Networks within and between systems

A system may contain networks of relationships among its components. Systems may also be connected through relationships that extend beyond their individual boundaries. A network may therefore exist within a system, link several systems, or connect elements across different manifestations.

Such networks may overlap, share elements or form nested arrangements. Their boundaries and membership may be clear in some cases and difficult to determine in others.

Changing and dynamic relationships

Relationships and networks may change as elements appear, disappear, move, develop or interact. Connections may form, strengthen, weaken, be interrupted or be replaced. A network's organization may therefore be dynamic rather than fixed.

Changes may occur at different rates and scales. Some network properties may remain relatively stable while individual connections change; in other cases, a small number of changes may reorganize the network substantially. These changes can be examined alongside Transformation & Emergence.

Networks, influence and emergence

The arrangement of relationships may affect how influence, information, resources or other forms of exchange move through a network. The effects may depend on the type of connection, the organization of the network, the properties of its elements and the conditions surrounding it. Questions about influence and its effects are also addressed in Causality & Influence.

New relationships may also create new forms of coordination or organization. Whether a network contributes to the emergence or origination of a new system, and what conditions are required, remain open questions for investigation.

Observing and describing networks

Network research requires decisions about what counts as an element, what qualifies as a connection, and which relationships are relevant. Different definitions can produce different network descriptions, even when they concern the same subject.

Research should make its selection criteria, observation period, data sources and limits explicit. Missing, hidden or changing relationships may affect the interpretation of network structure, and a representation should not be mistaken for the whole reality it is intended to describe. The choices and limits involved in quantifying observations are considered in Measurement & Quantification.

Research directions

  • Clarifying the distinctions among relationship, connection, association, dependency and interaction.
  • Identifying direct, indirect, reciprocal, asymmetric and multi-participant relationships.
  • Investigating necessary, conditional, partial and mutual dependencies.
  • Developing criteria for identifying network elements and the connections among them.
  • Comparing centralized, distributed, clustered, hierarchical and other network organizations.
  • Examining networks within systems and networks connecting different systems or manifestations.
  • Investigating how relationships and network structures form, change, persist or dissolve.
  • Studying how network organization relates to the movement of influence, information or resources.
  • Examining whether and how networks contribute to coordination, transformation, emergence or origination.
  • Determining how observation methods, incomplete data and chosen definitions affect network descriptions.
Research status: This page establishes working distinctions and research directions. It does not assume that every association represents a causal relationship, that network structure determines outcomes, or that one network model applies to all manifestations and systems. Definitions, mechanisms and evidence remain open to examination and revision.
