Constructed Realities and Games

Examining how an organized construct may establish a coherent domain of experience, operation and possibility.

Scope

This subject investigates constructed realities and games as organized domains. A domain may have its own rules, boundaries, conditions, structures, possible states, interactions and outcomes. The investigation considers how such a domain is established, how it operates, and how its participants or constituent systems relate to what lies beyond its boundaries.

The terms constructed reality and game are used here as broad research concepts. They are not restricted to digital simulations, entertainment or computer-generated environments.

What is a constructed reality?

A constructed reality is considered as a domain whose organization establishes a particular structure of existence or experience. Its construction may involve defining entities, relationships, rules, conditions, boundaries and the ways in which events or changes can occur.

A constructed domain may be internally coherent. Its rules and conditions can be meaningful within that domain even when they differ from those of another domain. Internal consistency does not, by itself, establish that the domain is the totality of existence or that it is independent of any wider source.

What is a game?

For this research, a game is examined as an organized domain in which rules, boundaries, conditions and possibilities shape what can occur. It may include participants, roles, actions, interactions, states and outcomes. A game may also contain systems or smaller domains with their own operating conditions.

This broad use of the term does not imply that every game is deceptive, artificial or a Fake Origin construct. Those are separate questions that require specific criteria.

Construction, operation and experience

It is useful to distinguish the establishment of a domain from its subsequent operation and from the experience of entities within it:

  • Construction: the establishment or origination of the domain, including its organization, rules, boundaries and initial possibilities.
  • Operation: the processes, interactions and changes that occur under the domain's conditions.
  • Experience: how participants or systems perceive, access and interpret the domain and its events.

These aspects may influence one another, but they are not interchangeable. A participant's experience may reveal only part of a domain's structure. Conversely, describing a domain's rules does not by itself establish how every participant experiences them.

Boundaries and internal rules

Boundaries may define what belongs to a domain, which interactions are possible, what information is accessible and which conditions apply. Rules may determine how states change or how actions produce outcomes. Together, they can make a domain appear self-contained from within.

A boundary can be operationally effective without being an absolute boundary of existence. Likewise, rules that govern one domain need not govern every other domain. The relationship between internal conditions and any wider context remains an important subject of investigation. See Boundaries and Domains for the broader study of boundary scope and operation.

Constructed domains and origination

The establishment of a new game or constructed reality may be examined as a possible form of origination: the coming-about of a new manifestation or organized domain. This should be distinguished from generating content within an existing game, changing its rules, or transforming something already present within it.

A constructed domain may also contain systems capable of generating outputs or originating further manifestations. Whether a particular process constitutes generation, transformation or origination must be examined rather than assumed from the fact that something new or unexpected appears.

See Creation of New Games and Origination for related investigations.

Relationship to True Origin and Fake Origin

Within the proposed Science of Origin framework, True Origin is the Oneness Origin. Fake Origin is a particular proposed manifestation that imitates Origin and presents relative independence as absolute independence.

Constructed realities and games provide a broader subject of investigation. A domain may be constructed, bounded or internally self-consistent without necessarily being Fake Origin. The specific claim that a construct imitates Origin, or presents itself as an absolutely independent source, requires separate examination.

The framework therefore does not treat every game, simulation, model or bounded reality as Fake Origin. The relationship between a particular construct and its proposed source must be considered on its own terms. The more specific proposal is examined in Fake Origin — The Divided Origin; resemblance to Origin is considered separately in Origin and Its Imitation.

Nested and connected domains

A constructed reality may contain multiple systems or subdomains. Domains may be nested, overlap, interact or remain partly separate. Their rules may be compatible in some respects and incompatible in others, while interactions may still occur across their boundaries.

These arrangements raise questions about how domains relate, whether one domain can establish another, and whether a domain can develop capabilities to originate further domains. Such possibilities remain research directions, not conclusions implied by the term constructed reality.

Research questions

  • What criteria distinguish a constructed reality from other forms of organized manifestation?
  • Which features are necessary to describe a domain as a game in this broad research sense?
  • How are a domain's rules, boundaries, conditions and possibilities established?
  • How can construction, operation and participant experience be examined separately?
  • What distinguishes a domain's internal boundaries from boundaries of existence as a whole?
  • How can nested, overlapping or connected domains be described and investigated?
  • What distinguishes generating content within a domain from originating a new domain?
  • Under what criteria, if any, would a constructed domain qualify as an imitation of Origin?
  • How can claims about a domain's source or independence be examined without assuming the conclusion?
  • Which aspects can be investigated through observation, measurement or reproducible procedures, and which remain conceptual?
