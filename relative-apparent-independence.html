Relative and Apparent Independence

Examining the difference between the independence a manifestation or system demonstrates within defined conditions and the broader independence it may appear to possess.

Scope

This subject examines independence as a structural relationship among manifestations, systems, components and their surrounding conditions. It asks how far a system can operate or maintain itself, what relationships remain relevant, and how the appearance of independence may differ from the independence that has actually been established.

The focus is general and analytical. It is not necessary to assume that an appearance of independence is deliberately created or deceptive. The proposed Fake Origin model's specific interpretation of simulated absolute independence is examined separately in Simulated Absolute Independence.

Relative independence

Relative independence describes independence within a stated scope, dimension or set of conditions. A system may perform particular functions, regulate aspects of its operation or maintain its organization without direct support from another system, while still relying on other conditions or relationships.

Relative independence is therefore meaningful rather than illusory by definition. A system's capacity to operate autonomously in a particular respect can be real and consequential even when it is not independent in every respect.

Any description of relative independence should specify the system, the reference point, the dimension being considered and the conditions under which the independence is observed.

Apparent independence

Apparent independence describes how independent a system seems from a particular perspective or on the basis of available observations. The appearance may accurately reflect a limited form of independence, or it may suggest a broader independence than the observations establish.

Apparent independence is not necessarily false. A system may genuinely operate without visible assistance in the circumstances observed. However, the absence of visible assistance does not establish the absence of indirect, background, intermittent or otherwise unobserved dependencies.

The distinction is between what a system appears able to do, what has been observed under specified conditions, and what is being claimed about its wider independence.

Independence depends on the reference point

A system may be independent of one particular component, resource or other system while remaining dependent on a wider environment. It may operate without direct intervention from its originating system but still rely on conditions established by that system or by other relationships.

Statements such as “independent of” or “self-sufficient” are incomplete unless the relevant reference point and scope are identified. Independence from one source should not be generalized into independence from all sources.

Dimensions of independence

Independence may be examined across several dimensions, which need not coincide:

  • Existence: whether the system can continue to exist without a specified source or support.
  • Operation: whether it can perform particular processes without direct external intervention.
  • Resources: whether it can obtain or maintain the resources needed for its operation.
  • Regulation: whether it can control or adjust aspects of its own activity.
  • Information: whether it can access, process or maintain the information needed for its functions.
  • Origination: whether it can originate further manifestations or systems, and under what conditions.

A system may show substantial independence in one dimension and clear dependence in another. These dimensions should be evaluated separately before drawing an overall conclusion.

Autonomy, self-maintenance and independence

Autonomy concerns a system's capacity to regulate or direct aspects of its own operation. Self-maintenance concerns processes that preserve or restore conditions required for continuation. Both may support relative independence, but neither automatically establishes complete independence.

A system may maintain itself while depending on external energy, materials, information or environmental conditions. It may also operate autonomously within rules or constraints that it did not establish. The source and scope of those conditions remain relevant to any assessment of independence.

Origin and subsequent independence

The source through which a manifestation or system comes about and its subsequent capacity to operate are distinct questions. A system may originate through another manifestation and later develop capabilities that allow it to function with increasing autonomy.

Conversely, a system's ability to originate further manifestations does not by itself demonstrate that it is independent of its own originating conditions. Origination capability, operational autonomy and independence from a source should not be treated as equivalent.

See Origination and Independent Origination Capabilities for these related but distinct questions.

How apparent independence may arise

Several circumstances may make a system appear more independent than the available evidence warrants. These are possibilities to examine, not assumptions about every system:

  • Dependencies may be indirect, distributed or outside the observer's field of access.
  • Support may be intermittent, so the system appears self-sustaining between periods of assistance.
  • External conditions may be stable and therefore go unnoticed as dependencies.
  • A system may have become autonomous in one function while retaining dependencies in others.
  • The observer may define the system boundary too narrowly to include relevant relationships.
  • Measurements may cover too short a period or too limited a range of operating conditions.

These possibilities should be tested against the actual system. They do not establish that a hidden dependency must exist, nor that apparent independence is necessarily misleading.

Examining claims of independence

A careful assessment begins by defining the system and the claim. It then identifies the relevant reference point, conditions, time span and dimensions of independence. Where feasible, the proposed dependencies can be examined by changing or removing particular inputs, supports or relationships while observing the effects.

Such tests must consider indirect dependencies, adaptation, delayed effects and alternative explanations. Failure to observe dependence in a limited test supports only a limited conclusion; it does not prove independence under every possible condition.

It is also important to distinguish evidence of operational autonomy from claims about ultimate sourcehood. The latter is a much broader proposition and cannot be established merely by observing that a system functions independently in selected respects.

Relationship to other subjects

This subject provides general structural distinctions that connect with, but do not replace, other investigations:

  • Dependence and Independence: examines forms, degrees and dimensions of reliance and autonomy.
  • Simulated Absolute Independence: examines the specific proposed Fake Origin model in which relative independence is presented as absolute independence.
  • Fabricated Division and Boundaries: examines how distinctions and boundaries may be presented as absolute separation within that model.
  • Independent Origination Capabilities: examines whether a manifestation or system can develop and exercise its own capability to originate further manifestations.

These subjects address different questions. A finding of relative autonomy does not establish simulated absolute independence, fabricated division or the presence of Fake Origin.

Research questions

  • What criteria are needed to describe a system as relatively independent in a particular dimension?
  • How should apparent independence be distinguished from independence demonstrated through observation or testing?
  • How does the choice of system boundary affect the assessment of independence?
  • Which dependencies may be indirect, intermittent, distributed or dependent on environmental conditions?
  • How do autonomy and self-maintenance contribute to relative independence?
  • Can independence in one dimension coexist with dependence in another, and how can both be measured?
  • How does a system's independence change over time or under different operating conditions?
  • What tests can distinguish the absence of observed dependence from evidence of independence?
  • How should operational independence be distinguished from claims about ultimate sourcehood?
  • Which aspects of relative and apparent independence can be investigated through reproducible methods?

Research status: This page establishes working distinctions for examining relative and apparent independence. It does not assume that apparent independence is false, that hidden dependencies must exist, or that operational autonomy establishes absolute independence or ultimate sourcehood. Definitions, criteria, methods and evidence remain open to examination and revision.
