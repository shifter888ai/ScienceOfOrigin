Causality & Influence

An open investigation of how causes, conditions and relationships may produce effects, and whether causality operates differently within and across systems.

The nature of causality

Causality concerns the relationship between what contributes to an event or change and the effect that follows. Influence may be direct or indirect, strong or weak, immediate or delayed. The precise meaning of cause, effect and influence requires careful definition in each area of investigation.

A sequence in which one event follows another does not, by itself, establish that the first event caused the second. Research must distinguish temporal order, association, contribution and causation.

Cause and effect

A cause may be understood as something that produces, enables, constrains or contributes to an effect. An effect may be a change in a property, a system state, a relationship or the origination of something new.

Not every effect necessarily has one identifiable cause. An outcome may depend on several contributing conditions, and the same apparent cause may produce different effects under different circumstances.

Direct and indirect influence

Some influences may act directly between two manifestations or events. Others may operate through intermediate processes, systems or chains of events. An influence may persist after its original source has changed or ceased to exist.

Tracing indirect influence requires distinguishing an immediate cause from earlier contributing events. The length and complexity of a causal chain may make attribution difficult without making the investigation meaningless.

Multiple causes and contributing conditions

An effect may arise only when several causes or conditions are present together. Some conditions may be necessary, others sufficient in a particular context, and others may alter the likelihood, magnitude or form of an outcome.

Research should identify which factors are causal, which are enabling conditions, and which are merely correlated with the effect. These distinctions should be supported by the method and evidence available.

Reciprocal and mutual causation

Two manifestations or systems may influence one another. A change in one may affect the other, which may then produce a further change in the first. Such reciprocal influence can create ongoing relationships rather than a one-way sequence.

Mutual influence does not necessarily mean that both directions operate in the same way, at the same strength or on the same timescale. Each direction may require separate investigation.

Linear and nonlinear causality

Some causal relationships may be relatively simple: a change in one factor produces a proportional or predictable change in another. Other relationships may be nonlinear, involving thresholds, delays, amplification, suppression or outcomes that depend sensitively on initial conditions.

A complex or unpredictable outcome should not automatically be treated as uncaused. Equally, apparent complexity should not be used to assume hidden causes without evidence.

Causality within systems

Within a system, principles and rules may constrain which events can influence others and how effects occur. A causal explanation that works within one system may depend on conditions specific to that system.

Research can examine whether causal relationships remain stable across different states and conditions, and whether changes to a system alter the relationships between its components.

Causality across different systems

Different systems may operate according to different or incompatible principles and rules while still interacting. It is therefore worth investigating how an influence can pass between systems, how an effect is expressed, and whether either system provides a sufficient account of the resulting event.

An effect produced through interaction between systems may not be fully explained by the causal principles of either system considered separately. This is an open research question, not an established conclusion.

Feedback and causal cycles

Feedback occurs when the effects of a process influence its subsequent operation. Positive feedback may reinforce a change, while negative feedback may counteract or regulate it. Feedback can create cycles, sustained patterns or changing system behavior.

In a causal cycle, the distinction between cause and effect may depend on the point in the cycle and the timescale being examined. A useful account should describe the sequence and conditions rather than force every relationship into a single starting point.

Origination and causality

Origination concerns the process through which manifestations originate. A central question is whether origination follows the same causal principles as processes occurring within manifestations, or whether it represents a fundamentally different kind of relationship.

The framework also considers origination as potentially spontaneous and continuous. How this understanding relates to cause, intention, conditions and effects remains open for clarification and investigation.

Beyond known causal relationships

Current descriptions of causality may not encompass every possible relationship among manifestations and systems. Unfamiliar forms of influence or origination may require new concepts and methods of investigation.

Remaining open to such possibilities does not mean treating them as facts. Proposed causal relationships should be distinguished from observed sequences, interpretations and findings supported by evidence.

Research directions

  • Clarifying the meanings of cause, effect, influence, condition and contribution.
  • Distinguishing causation from temporal sequence, association and correlation.
  • Investigating direct, indirect, multiple and reciprocal causal relationships.
  • Examining nonlinear effects, delays, thresholds and feedback cycles.
  • Studying how principles and rules shape causal relationships within systems.
  • Investigating influence and causality between systems with different or incompatible principles and rules.
  • Examining whether interactions between systems can produce effects not explained by either system alone.
  • Clarifying the relationship between Origination and causality.
  • Identifying which causal claims can be observed, measured, tested or independently verified.
Research status: This page records concepts and questions for investigation. It does not assume that every sequence establishes causation, that all effects have a single cause, or that one causal model applies universally. Proposed relationships require methods and evidence appropriate to the claims being made.
