III · Structure

Systems

An exploration of what constitutes a system, how systems are organized and bounded, how they relate and interact, and how they persist, transform or give rise to new systems.

What is a system?

In the developing Science of Origin framework, a system is considered as an organized arrangement of components, relationships or processes that can be examined as a connected whole. This is a working description, not a final definition.

A system may be a manifestation in its own right, or it may be identified as an arrangement within a larger manifestation. The relationship between a manifestation and a system within it remains an important question. The terms should not be treated as interchangeable without examining the context.

Not every collection of things is necessarily a system in the same sense. Some arrangements may have identifiable interactions, dependencies or organization; others may be grouped together primarily for the convenience of an observer. Research should distinguish organization that can be demonstrated from a system boundary or category introduced for analysis.

Identifying a system

A system may be recognized through relationships among its components, coordinated or interdependent processes, recurring interactions, shared conditions or an identifiable organization. No single feature is assumed to be necessary in every case.

What counts as one system may depend partly on the question being investigated and the scale of observation. A useful analytical boundary does not automatically establish an absolute division in existence. Conversely, an observer-defined description may still refer to real interactions or functional organization.

Any proposed system should therefore be described with its criteria, scope and purpose made clear. Researchers should ask what evidence supports treating the arrangement as a system and what relevant relationships may have been excluded by the chosen description.

System boundaries and surroundings

A system may be distinguished by its particular organization, relationships, processes or operating principles. Its boundary may be clear, shared, overlapping, permeable, conditional or difficult to identify.

A boundary may regulate exchange, mark an interface or define the scope within which particular processes and rules operate. It may be operationally effective without making the system absolutely separate from its surroundings.

A system may exchange matter, energy, information or influence with other systems or with its surroundings. Some relationships may be direct; others may pass through intermediaries. Whether a boundary is physical, functional, informational, conceptual or defined for measurement should be specified rather than assumed.

These questions connect with Boundaries and Domains and Fabricated Boundaries and Simulated Separation.

Components and internal organization

A system may contain components, relationships and processes that together form its internal organization. Some components may themselves be systems, creating nested or layered arrangements.

The organization of a system may be as important to its character as the individual components it contains. How components relate, influence one another and contribute to the whole is a central subject for investigation.

Components need not be identical, interchangeable or equally important. Some may be essential to particular functions; others may be replaceable, temporary or relevant only under certain conditions. These distinctions should be established for each system rather than generalized.

Multiple, nested and interconnected systems

A manifestation may contain multiple systems. Systems may be independent in some respects while remaining connected or interdependent in others. One system may exist within another, and systems may overlap, share components or participate in common networks.

These arrangements need not form a single simple hierarchy. A component may participate in several systems at once, and a system may be part of a larger arrangement without being wholly defined by that larger arrangement.

Terms such as part, whole, subsystem and environment describe relationships from a particular perspective or level of analysis. Their usefulness does not establish that every such division is absolute or universal.

Principles, rules and system conditions

Systems may operate under particular principles, rules and conditions. Different systems may have different operating rules, including rules that appear incompatible. The rules observed in one system should not automatically be assumed to govern another system, even when both exist within one manifestation.

A system's operating conditions may enable some processes and constrain others. They may also change through internal development or interaction with other systems. A change in conditions or rules does not by itself establish that a new system has originated.

The distinction between system-level rules and human-made conventions is important. A classification used to describe a system may be useful without being an intrinsic feature of the arrangement itself. See Principles and Rules and Conditions and Constraints.

System interactions

Systems may interact and influence one another even when they operate according to different or apparently incompatible principles and rules. The conditions that permit such interactions, and the effects they produce, remain important research questions.

Interactions may preserve existing systems, change their relationships, alter their internal organization, transform their structures or contribute to the origination of new systems. Interaction should not automatically be equated with dependence, causation or the creation of a new system; each relationship requires its own examination.

Systems may also interact indirectly through shared components, environments, intermediaries or wider networks. An absence of direct interaction does not establish an absence of every possible relationship, while a possible indirect pathway does not prove that an interaction actually occurs.

Identity, continuity and transformation

A system may change its components, relationships, processes or properties while continuing to be regarded as the same system. At some point, however, a transformation may be so fundamental that the resulting arrangement is better considered a different system.

How to distinguish continuity from the origination of a different system is not yet settled. It may require examining which characteristics remain, which relationships persist, whether the organization has changed, and whether the system's capabilities or operating principles have fundamentally altered.

Identity may also depend on the criteria used to track a system. Different descriptions may emphasize continuity of components, organization, function, relationships or history. These criteria should be made explicit rather than treated as automatically equivalent. This subject connects with Identity and Continuity and Transformation and Emergence.

Emergence and the origination of new systems

Interactions among systems may contribute to the emergence or origination of new systems. A resulting system may have an organization, principles, rules or capabilities that differ from those of the systems involved.

A new system may not be adequately described as a simple sum of its contributing components. Its organization and relationships may produce characteristics that are not present in the same way in any component considered separately. Whether a particular case represents emergence, transformation or origination requires clear criteria and evidence.

Within the developing framework, an emergent system may also develop its own capability for origination. Its subsequent development could therefore lead to forms of systems and origination that are not simply repetitions of those of its originating systems. These are possibilities for investigation, not established empirical conclusions.

Systems and the scope of Origin

In the Science of Origin framework, systems are examined as arrangements within manifestations or as manifestations in their own right. Their boundaries, operating rules and degrees of independence should not automatically be treated as limits on Origin itself.

This distinction does not settle the nature of any particular system. It sets out a conceptual question: whether a condition that applies within a system can legitimately be generalized to the scope of existence or to Origin. A system-level limitation should not be assumed to establish an absolute limitation beyond its demonstrated context.

Research directions

  • Developing a clearer working description of a system and its relationship to a manifestation.
  • Identifying criteria and evidence for distinguishing an organized system from an observer-defined grouping.
  • Investigating how system boundaries can be recognized, described and tested.
  • Examining components, internal relationships, processes and nested structures.
  • Studying overlapping, interconnected, interdependent and relatively independent systems.
  • Exploring interactions between systems with different or apparently incompatible principles and rules.
  • Distinguishing direct interaction, indirect relationships, dependence, influence and causation.
  • Investigating how systems maintain identity and continuity through changes in components and organization.
  • Distinguishing system development, transformation, emergence and the origination of a new system.
  • Examining whether interactions can give rise to systems with new principles, rules or origination capabilities.
  • Identifying which aspects of systems can be observed, described, measured or experimentally investigated.
